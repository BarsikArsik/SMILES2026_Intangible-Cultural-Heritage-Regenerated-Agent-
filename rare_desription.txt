Folk decorative art traditionally encompasses regions of the Far East.

It was divided into women's and men's. Women were involved in the artistic

processing of items from soft materials such as fish skin, fur, hides, fabrics, and also
from birch bark, i.e., they made clothing, footwear, and utensils. For practical needs,
fish skin has been used for centuries. The processes of dressing and sewing are extremely labor-intensive.

The festive robe of the Nai craftswoman N. Belda (Sakhalin), the back of which
is sewn from rows of "scales," features embroidery executed on six different sides
with decorative stitches.
Of particular interest are L. D. Kimova's custom-made collectible dolls in the traditional style
of Nivkh clothing. In her works, the craftswoman uses traditional materials: fish
skins, seal and mink fur, birch bark, as well as techniques favored by Far Eastern artisans: appliqué, inlay, and embroidery. The doll collection was purchased from museums
by the craftswoman. She donated one of them (the composition "Fishermen") to the museum, as well
as the collage "Yukola Drying" and a birch bark container. The museum also acquired from her a festive
Nivkh robe, a collage called "Keraf of a Nivkh Summer Dwelling," Nivkh decorative handbags, and a set of earmuffs and armlets. In creating the collage "Keraf of a Nivkh Summer Dwelling," the "Nivkh craftswoman" uses not only colors but also various parts of the fish.

The Nivkh artist N. Pulus is a traditional artist who uses materials in her work. The museum's collection includes her ornamental and narrative panels: "Nivkh Ornament," "Bear and Chipmunk," and "Bear and Squirrel."
V. Osipova, a Sakhalin artist, developed a unique painting technique. A native of the Uilta culture, she introduces ethnographic elements into her paintings, and, if the piece has a national flavor, she adds a touch of authenticity. In her panel "Girl and Heron," fine graphic lines
combined with blurred ink stains depict a northern landscape, a girl in the desert
suit standing amid tall grass, a river behind her, a boat with a person sitting on it, and a bird flying in the sky. A pen and ink painting on fish skin,

Headdresses, traditional in form, color scheme, and ornamentation,
mittens made of fabric and rovduga (tanned reindeer skin), and a torbasa (a traditional Russian brocade cap) are distinguished by their high craftsmanship and the extraordinary delicacy of their decorative finish. In particular, the Evenki handbag and gloves utilize a unique embroidery technique using reindeer neck hair. This is an ancient traditional technique, rare and almost forgotten today [4, p. 113]. The handbag was purchased from L. Letskikh, a resident of Korsakov, and the gloves from the Khabarovsk art and production workshops.
The Negidal mittens (designed by M.K. Okhlopkova), decorated with magnificent embroidery, also came from there. A rosette with floral and geometric elements is incorporated into an arched border with a variegated outline. The bright colors of the threads stand out against the light tan background. The cuffs are decorated with the same elements, and their edges are adorned with fur stripes.
A Nanai festive robe, a torbasa, and summer mittens characterize the Nanai craftswoman N.D. Beldy as a great artist, possessing an innate sense of color and compositional flair, as a master of national artistic techniques, and as an expert in national artistic and aesthetic traditions.
